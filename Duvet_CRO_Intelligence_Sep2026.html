First month findings and plan of action

Mobile visitors are getting stuck before they buy.

One month of GA4 and Microsoft Clarity data, plus our own testing of the site, shows where Duvet.co.za loses visitors, what is holding tracking back, and the plan we will follow over the next six months to improve conversion.

About this data. This report is based on one month of GA4 and Clarity data. Executive summary

Three things the data and our testing are telling us

01

Mobile is where the sale is lost

Mobile users view fewer products, add far fewer of them to the cart (9.5% against 53.1% on desktop), and none completed a purchase in the tracked funnel. Desktop cart abandonment (69.2%) sits close to the 70.22% average that Baymard Institute reports across 50 studies. Every tracked mobile cart was abandoned, although that rests on only 8 carts, so treat the comparison as directional. The clearest gap is earlier: getting mobile visitors from a product page into the cart.

02

Our tracking is not yet capturing everything

GA4 is missing a large share of sessions, credits 92% of revenue to Direct (a sign that attribution is incomplete), and does not record the shipping and payment steps of checkout. Until this is fixed, every conversion figure is a minimum and revenue opportunity cannot be sized with confidence.

03

Checkout has errors at the point of payment

Testing as a guest, choosing Ozow failed with a “did not return a redirect URL” message, and cancelling on the payment page led to a server error. PayFast worked, and checkout does not require login. We do not yet know whether Ozow fails for customers, so this needs looking into and re-testing on real devices.

Our own testing

What we saw when we tested the site ourselves

These are single manual tests, each to be re-tested on real Android and iPhone devices. Personal details have been removed from the screenshots.

Hero on a large desktop monitor

The “50% OFF” message is cut off at the bottom of the hero and the line beneath “SALE” is hidden. It displays fully on a laptop screen. There is no visible button or link, and the cookie banner sits in the corner over the product row.

Manual test · desktop
Hero on a phone

The sale hero fits the screen but is only an image, with no link or button. The small print (available online and in stores, while stocks last, terms apply) is very small. The offer is available online, so linking the hero to the sale page is safe.

Manual test · mobile
Login and account page on mobile

After logging in on a phone we were sent to the homepage, including when we logged in from checkout. The cart kept its items, so we could go back through the cart icon, but not automatically. Opening the account page then showed a two column layout that does not fit the screen, with the main content cut off on the right.

Login is optional, so the effect on sales is likely smaller than a forced login would be, but visitors who choose to log in during checkout are sent away from it.

Manual test · mobile
Payment and checkout (guest test)

Testing as a guest, choosing Ozow showed a browser message: “Payment gateway did not return a redirect URL. Please try another payment method.” PayFast worked and offered a wide range of options, including cards, instant EFT, Google Pay, SnapScan and Zapper.

Cancelling on the payment page led to a 500 server error at duvet.co.za/payment/cancel/24, not back to checkout. One of the four payment options failed in our guest test and a shopper who cancels reaches an error page. Both sit at the point of purchase and need looking into. We do not yet know whether Ozow fails for customers.

Manual test · desktop, guest checkout
These signals are consistent with a mobile experience where product pages and add to cart do not work smoothly, but the recordings still need to be reviewed to confirm exactly what visitors are tapping. We have shortlisted four sessions to watch first.
Opportunity

What the mobile gap could be worth

The most reliable way to size the mobile gap is to ask what would happen if mobile converted at a fraction of desktop's rate. We use the 299 measured mobile users and one order per new purchaser. Because GA4 is undercounting, all figures are a floor, and rand values will be added once order figures are reconciled with store totals in October.

ScenarioMobile conversionExtra purchasers in 28 days
Modest recovery2.0%6
Half of desktop's rate3.35%10
Parity with desktop (ceiling)6.7%20

Mobile usually converts below desktop, so we treat the middle scenario as the reasonable planning case and parity as a ceiling. If mobile product views matched desktop (40.8% against 28.1%), about 38 more mobile users would reach a product page each month. Desktop's 6.7% conversion rests on 8 purchases and includes the launch-week orders, so we would not build a target on it.

Ways of working

How we will know a change worked

We do not set targets on one month of data. Instead, each change is logged with its date, and the following month's report compares the related measure with the September baseline in this report and with the month before. For example, after the hero is linked we look at taps and dead clicks on it, and after the mobile fixes we look at the mobile add-to-cart rate. Because volumes are small, we look for movement that is consistent over several months, not a single month's change.

Each month

  • Check the data. Compare GA4 with Clarity, and with order totals from the store where available, so we know how much of the picture we can trust.
  • Watch what visitors do. Review Clarity recordings and heatmaps and the funnel by device.
  • Ship fixes and, when traffic allows, tests. In the order set out in the plan of action.
  • Log every change. Record what changed and on what date, so the next report can show whether the related measure moved.
  • Report. A monthly report shows what changed, what we learned and what happens next.
  • Agree next month's priorities.

What each monthly report will track

  • Tracking health: GA4 sessions against Clarity sessions, and GA4 orders against order totals from the store where available.
  • The mobile and desktop funnel at each step, especially the mobile add-to-cart rate and checkout completion once the checkout events are recorded.
  • Orders, revenue and average order value, with the mix of large and small orders.
  • Channel and paid traffic quality, cross-referenced with campaign data from October.
  • Clarity behaviour signals: dead clicks, quick backs, rage clicks, JavaScript errors and interaction speed.

A note on testing

At today's measured volume, A/B testing purchase conversion is not realistic. Detecting a lift from 1.5% to 1.8% needs about 28,000 sessions per variant. Even testing the add-to-cart rate on mobile product pages, from 9.5% to 15%, needs about 560 product viewers per variant, while the measured mobile volume is 84 in four weeks. Until traffic and tracking allow it, we release fixes and verify them with before and after comparisons and Clarity recordings, and we test formally once corrected traffic makes it worthwhile.

Data notes

How to read the numbers

  • Sources and windows. GA4 for duvet.co.za: sessions, users and the funnel cover 2 to 29 September 2026 (28 days) and orders cover 1 to 30 September. Microsoft Clarity project Duvet South Africa: exports cover 1 to 30 September and dashboard figures cover about 28 to 30 September.
  • Funnel definition. The GA4 purchase journey counts users who complete the steps in order. It shows 8 purchasers while overall reporting shows 11, the difference being people who reached /checkout directly.
  • Sample size. Several mobile stages rest on single-digit counts (8 add to carts, 2 checkout starts). The direction is clear, the exact percentages are not.
  • Benchmarks. The 70.22% average cart abandonment rate (50 studies, updated 22 September 2025) and the reasons-for-abandonment figure (US online shoppers, excluding those just browsing) come from Baymard Institute, baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate. They are general ecommerce figures, not specific to South African home textiles.
  • Order value and mix. GA4 shows 22 orders from 11 purchasers. One order (R6,892) is 31% of revenue and the three largest are 49%, so the average is pulled up by a few large orders. 19 of the 22 orders fall between 3 and 7 September. Order figures will be reconciled with store totals from October, and rand values for the opportunity will be added then.
  • Limited history. The report covers the first month of the engagement, so there is no month-on-month comparison. Monthly reports from October will measure movement against this baseline, and we recommend keeping that up for 3 to 6 months before drawing firm conclusions about typical behaviour.
  • Checks planned for October. A cookie consent test in a private window, re-testing payment methods and checkout on real devices, and a comparison of GA4 orders with order totals from the store.
  • To add from October. Google Ads and other paid campaign data, cross-referenced against these findings, and true exit page data from Clarity.

Plain-language guide

GA4 (Google Analytics 4)
Google's tool for counting visits, sales and how visitors move through the site.
Microsoft Clarity
A free tool that records anonymised visitor sessions and shows where people tap, scroll and get stuck.
Session and user
A session is one visit. A user is one person, who may visit more than once.
Funnel
The steps a visitor takes towards a purchase: view a product, add to cart, begin checkout, purchase.
Cart abandonment
Adding something to the cart and leaving without buying.
Dead click
A tap or click on something that does nothing.
Rage click
Repeated fast taps on the same spot, usually a sign of frustration.
Quick back
Landing on a page and going straight back to the previous one.
Consent mode
A setting that controls whether analytics tools may record a visitor depending on their cookie choice.
Tag Manager (GTM)
The tool that switches analytics and advertising tags on and off on the site.
Key events
Actions GA4 is told to treat as important, such as a purchase.
INP (Interaction to Next Paint)
How quickly the page responds after a tap. Under 200 milliseconds is considered good.
